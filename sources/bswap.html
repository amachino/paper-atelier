Abstract

Far-detuned frequency allocations in fixed-frequency superconducting processors mitigate spectral crowding but constrain cross-resonance interactions at practical drive amplitudes. Here, we demonstrate that a single strong drive induces a large X​X−Y​YXX-YY interaction in the far-detuned regime, while a weak second tone tunes the accompanying Z​ZZZ phase. The interaction realizes a 150150-ns bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} gate, which can directly prepare a maximally entangled state from the ground state. For the implementations on the same qubit pair at matched drive strengths, cross resonance gives a shorter CNOT operation, while bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} gives a 33%33\% shorter SWAP operation with a higher IRB fidelity. The multilevel theory further predicts a regime where stronger driving can yield a speed advantage for CNOT as well, a possibility absent in the two-level model. As an application of the X​X−Y​YXX-YY interaction, we also demonstrate two-spin squeezing beyond the standard quantum limit through two-axis countertwisting. The interaction realized here complements cross resonance and offers a route to efficient gate synthesis and quantum simulation of interacting spin systems.

I Introduction

Cross-resonance (CR) gates are widely used in fixed-frequency, fixed-coupling superconducting architectures, where driving one qubit at its neighbor’s transition frequency induces an effective Z​XZX interaction used to implement CNOT operations [1, 2, 3, 4]. Far-detuned frequency allocations can mitigate spectral crowding [5, 6], but larger qubit-frequency separations reduce the CR interaction strength for a given coupling and drive amplitude [7, 8, 9]. Practical upper limits on drive amplitude therefore motivate additional native gates that complement CR for more efficient compilation of a broader range of quantum circuits.

In addition to CR, a range of entangling gates has been proposed and demonstrated in fixed-frequency superconducting architectures. These approaches include conditional-phase gates [10, 11, 12, 13, 14] and population-exchange gates [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. The CZ gate realized through a conditional-phase interaction is locally equivalent to a CNOT operation, whereas iSWAP and bSWAP belong to a different local-equivalence class under the Cartan decomposition [20]. Native exchange gates therefore provide complementary building blocks for gate synthesis alongside CR [19, 21].

Stark iSWAP and bSWAP gates induce coherent population exchange within the odd- and even-parity subspaces, respectively. Both gates can be activated by microwave drives detuned from the individual qubit transitions [19]. Stark bSWAP is particularly attractive for far-detuned qubits because it can reach two-photon resonance without closing the large frequency gap. However, the fixed-frequency demonstration in Ref. 19 was performed in the straddling regime and used a two-level approximation for its analytical description. Whether Stark bSWAP gates could be realized under strong driving in far-detuned multilevel transmons remained untested.

Strong off-resonant driving of multilevel transmons can generate a substantial Z​ZZZ interaction alongside the desired bSWAP exchange. The resulting conditional phase must be compensated to realize an ideal bSWAP gate. Because the X​X−Y​YXX-YY and Z​ZZZ interactions commute, the Z​ZZZ phase can be controlled separately from the exchange. We exploit this property by combining the bSWAP drive with a second tone on the other qubit to tune Z​ZZZ through Stark-induced Z​ZZZ by level excursions (siZZle) [22, 23, 24]. This approach supports both ideal bSWAP gates and a tunable combination of X​X−Y​YXX-YY and Z​ZZZ interactions for quantum simulation [18].

In this work, we develop a multilevel dressed-state theory of these interactions and experimentally demonstrate bSWAP and bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} gates in a far-detuned transmon pair. We characterize the gates using randomized and cross-entropy benchmarking. We compare the durations and fidelities of CNOT and SWAP implementations based on CR or bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} on the same qubit pair at matched drive strengths, showing how these native gates complement each other. We also demonstrate two-spin squeezing beyond the standard quantum limit (SQL) using the X​X−Y​YXX-YY interaction.

Section III presents the experimental implementation, Z​ZZZ compensation, and gate benchmarking. Section IV discusses complementary native interactions and quantum-simulation applications, including two-spin squeezing. Section V summarizes the results. II.3 ZZ control with siZZle

In multilevel transmon qubits, the X​X−Y​YXX-YY interaction in Sec. II.2 is accompanied by a Z​ZZZ interaction. Its coefficient ζ\zeta contains both the static Z​ZZZ coupling and the contribution induced by the drive on QA\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{A}}. The interaction Hamiltonian can be written as

Hint=−JX​Y4​(X​X−Y​Y)+ζ4​Z​Z.H_{\mathrm{int}}=-\frac{J_{XY}}{4}(XX-YY)+\frac{\zeta}{4}ZZ{}. (16)

The expression for ζ\zeta is derived in Appendix A.2.

Since the X​X−Y​YXX-YY and Z​ZZZ terms commute, their evolutions can be treated separately. We add a second tone on QB\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{B}} at the bSWAP drive frequency ωd\omega_{\mathrm{d}} to control ζ\zeta through siZZle [23]. Its amplitude ΩB\Omega_{\mathrm{B}} and phase φ\varphi relative to the drive on QA\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{A}} serve as the control parameters for the Z​ZZZ interaction. With the drive on QA\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{A}} held fixed, a weak second tone changes the coefficient according to

ζ⁡(ΩB,φ)≃ζ⁡(0)+χZ​Z​ΩB​cos⁡φ,\zeta(\Omega_{\mathrm{B}},\varphi)\simeq\zeta(0)+\chi_{ZZ}\Omega_{\mathrm{B}}\cos\varphi, (17)

where ζ⁡(0)\zeta(0) is the value without the second tone and χZ​Z\chi_{ZZ} is derived in Appendix A.3. The phase selects the sign of the change and the amplitude sets its size. Choosing them so that ζ=0\zeta=0 removes the Z​ZZZ term and yields the ideal bSWAP Hamiltonian in Eq. (9).

The same control can also be used to enhance the Z​ZZZ interaction. The resulting operation combines even-parity exchange with a conditional phase. It is the bSWAP counterpart of fSim gates, which instead exchange populations within the odd-parity subspace [28]. It is locally equivalent to an X​X​ZXXZ-type evolution and offers a route to Hamiltonian engineering [18]. The local equivalence, anisotropy, and isotropic Heisenberg condition are given in Appendix D.

IV Discussion

IV.1 Complementary roles of bSWAP and CR

The benefit of a native interaction depends on the operation to be synthesized. CR supplies one Cartan axis, while bSWAP exchange supplies two equal axes. Their ideal interaction times therefore differ for CNOT and SWAP operations [39, 19]. The CNOT construction requires two bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} gates, compared with one RZ​X​(π2)R_{ZX}(\frac{\pi}{2}) gate for CR. The SWAP construction requires three native two-qubit gates in either case. These different gate counts lead to different conditions for a speed advantage (Appendix G.2).

The implementations of CNOT and SWAP on QA\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{A}}–QB\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{B}} show this target dependence (Table 2). The CR- and bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}}-based implementations are compared at matched drive amplitudes inferred from Stark shifts. CR gives a 26%26\% shorter CNOT with higher fidelity. The bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}}-based SWAP is 33%33\% shorter and also has higher fidelity. Selecting the native gate for each target operation can therefore make circuit compilation more efficient. Comparison details are given in Appendix G.

Frequency collisions provide another motivation for complementary native interactions [5, 6]. The CR drive is tied to the target-qubit transition, whereas the Stark bSWAP drive frequency and amplitude can be chosen together along the resonance curve. This freedom may help avoid unwanted resonances, with a trade-off in exchange rate and required drive amplitude.

Native bSWAP gates may also serve as building blocks for quantum error correction. Dynamic surface-code circuits have been demonstrated using iSWAP gates in place of conventional CNOT/CZ gates [40]. Since bSWAP is locally equivalent to iSWAP, analogous circuits could be implemented using native bSWAP gates and local single-qubit rotations. Compensating the accompanying Z​ZZZ phase is important for realizing the intended Clifford operation in such circuits.

IV.2 Multilevel effects on gate speed

The analytical description of native Stark bSWAP in Ref. 19 is based on a two-level model. Our multilevel treatment includes drive-induced mixing with higher transmon levels. With the same hardware limit on drive amplitude, the two-level model does not permit a bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}}-based construction with a shorter ideal CNOT interaction time than CR (Appendix G.2). Our multilevel model instead predicts this speed advantage at large qubit detuning and strong drive, where JX​Y>2​JZ​XJ_{XY}>2J_{ZX}. The matrix elements determining the bSWAP and CR coefficients respond differently to this mixing, which changes the gate-speed comparison (Fig. 16).

Realizing this predicted regime requires control hardware capable of delivering stronger drives with high amplitude stability. Amplitude fluctuations shift the dressed-state splitting, detuning the two-photon resonance and changing the accumulated local phases.

IV.3 Applications to quantum simulation

Native access to the X​X−Y​YXX-YY interaction provides a direct route to quantum simulation of spin-squeezing dynamics. For the two-qubit collective spin Sμ=(σμA+σμB)/2S_{\mu}=(\sigma_{\mu}^{\mathrm{A}}+\sigma_{\mu}^{\mathrm{B}})/2 with μ=x,y,z\mu=x,y,z, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (9) takes the two-axis countertwisting form [41],

HX​Y=−JX​Y2​(Sx2−Sy2).H_{XY}=-\frac{J_{XY}}{2}(S_{x}^{2}-S_{y}^{2}). (19)

Partial evolution from |00⟩\lvert 00\rangle can reduce transverse spin fluctuations while retaining a nonzero mean longitudinal spin.

The two-qubit measurements in Fig. 9 illustrate this application. We identify the squeezed spin direction using the method of Ref. [42]. An independent measurement along this direction yields a Wineland parameter [43] of ξR2=0.684\xi_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}{}=0.684, with a 95%95\% statistical interval of [0.644,0.729][0.644,0.729], below the coherent-state SQL of ξR2=1\xi_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}{}=1. This result uses initial-state heralding without readout-error mitigation. Preparation conditions and the definition and measurement of ξR2\xi_{\mathrm{R}}^{2} are given in Appendix F.

Control of the accompanying Z​ZZZ interaction also provides access to anisotropic spin models. On a phase-aligned bipartite lattice, a sublattice basis change maps X​X−Y​YXX-YY plus Z​ZZZ to X​X​ZXXZ interactions (Appendix D).

For example, collective spin squeezing has been predicted in short-range X​X​ZXXZ lattices for suitable anisotropies and initial states [44, 45]. Coherent averaging with isotropic exchange provides another proposed route to collective countertwisting within a protected spin manifold [46].

Realizing these applications would require driving many qubit pairs simultaneously. Accessing a wider range of interaction anisotropies would require stronger siZZle tones and joint calibration of the siZZle and bSWAP drives.

V Conclusion

We demonstrated Z​ZZZ-free Stark bSWAP gates in far-detuned, fixed-frequency transmons using a strong exchange drive and a weak tone to compensate the accompanying Z​ZZZ phase. The 278278-ns bSWAP had a fidelity of 98.78%98.78\% from IRB, and the 150150-ns bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} had a fidelity of 99.34%99.34\% from interleaved XEB. A single bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}} pulse directly prepared a Bell state from the ground state. The X​X−Y​YXX-YY interaction also generated two-spin squeezing below the SQL.

The CNOT and SWAP comparisons demonstrated the complementary roles of CR and bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}}. CR gave a shorter CNOT with higher fidelity, while the bSWAP\sqrt{\mathrm{bSWAP}}-based SWAP was 33%33\% shorter and also had higher fidelity. Selecting the native gate for each target operation can therefore make circuit compilation more efficient. Control of X​X−Y​YXX-YY and Z​ZZZ interactions also offers a route to quantum simulation of interacting spin systems.
